# 18. Stakeholder System Help

## 01. Stakeholder Categories Help

Stakeholder Categories groups the different kinds of people and organizations who affect or are affected by your organization.
List each category (customers, employees, suppliers, investors, regulators, community, and similar) with a short description.
Cover more than the obvious categories, including "Educational & Research Institutions," to reflect the full range of relationships that actually matter to the organization.
This is the starting point for identifying every stakeholder your organization needs to actively manage.

## 02. Stakeholder Register Help

The Stakeholder Register lists your actual, specific stakeholders and assigns ownership for each relationship.
For each stakeholder, note their category, the type of relationship (commercial, internal, regulatory, and similar), and who's responsible for managing it.
Pair each stakeholder group with one named responsible role, like Regulatory Authorities managed by the Compliance Manager, so nothing is left unassigned.
This register is what turns "we should manage this relationship" into someone's actual job.

## 03. Stakeholder Expectations Help

Stakeholder Expectations documents what each group actually needs from you, and what you're committing to in return.
For each stakeholder, note their expectations and your organizational commitment to meeting them.
Pair each expectation with a real, specific commitment, such as meeting employees' expectation of career development with an actual promise of professional development programs, rather than a vague reassurance.
This is what keeps your relationships built on stated, checkable commitments instead of assumptions.

## 04. Stakeholder Engagement Help

Stakeholder Engagement documents how you actually maintain each relationship, not just how often you'd like to.
For each stakeholder, list the engagement methods you use (meetings, surveys, workshops, and similar) and how frequently they happen.
Vary both method and frequency by stakeholder (weekly team meetings for employees versus annual community meetings) rather than a one-size-fits-all engagement plan.
Matching the method and cadence to each group is what keeps engagement genuinely useful instead of just going through the motions.

## 05. Stakeholder Communication Help

Stakeholder Communication documents what information each group actually receives, how, and how often.
For each stakeholder, define the purpose of communicating with them, the method you'll use, and the frequency.
Tailor both purpose and channel to each audience (an annual report and shareholder meeting for investors versus an intranet and newsletter for employees) rather than one blanket communication plan.
Getting this right is what makes sure the right information reaches the right people without over- or under-communicating.

## 06. Stakeholder Review Help

Stakeholder Review is how this whole System stays current: a regular check on whether your relationships are actually working.
Set a review schedule, define what the review evaluates (satisfaction, relationship quality, communication effectiveness, and similar), and list improvement actions.
Review on a set schedule but also trigger a review after significant organizational changes, rather than relying only on a fixed calendar.
This is what keeps your stakeholder relationships responsive to change instead of managed on autopilot.
