# 02. Brand System Help

## 01. Brand Kit Help

The Brand Kit is a quick-reference summary of your visual identity — the one-page version you'd hand to a new designer or partner.
Write a short Brand Summary describing the overall visual impression, then list 3–7 Brand Attributes and a handful of Brand Keywords that should guide design decisions.
Tie these together tightly: attributes like "Minimal" and "Natural" should be echoed directly in keywords like "Simplicity" and "Sustainability," so nothing feels disconnected.
This is the anchor every other section in this System should visibly connect back to.

## 02. Logo Specification Help

This section locks down exactly how your logo can and can't be used, so it stays recognizable no matter who's applying it.
List every approved logo variation (primary, icon, monochrome, light/dark versions), then define the minimum size, required clear space, and acceptable backgrounds.
Be exact — give precise pixel and millimeter minimums, and a clear list of "never" actions like stretching, rotating, or adding shadows.
The more concrete this section is, the fewer inconsistent logo uses you'll have to fix later.

## 03. Color System Help

The Color System documents your official brand colors so anyone building anything on your behalf uses the exact same palette.
For each primary, secondary, and neutral color, record a name, a HEX code, and when it should be used.
Name each color and give it an exact code, like "Forest Green — #234D36 — primary brand color, headers, highlights, buttons," rather than leaving it as an unlabeled swatch.
Keeping this precise is what prevents color drift across your website, products, and marketing materials.

## 04. Typography System Help

The Typography System defines which fonts you use and where, so text looks consistent everywhere it appears.
Name a primary typeface for headings and interface text, and optionally a secondary or technical typeface for other contexts, then define what each heading level and body text is used for.
Keep each typeface's role distinct and purposeful — for instance, a serif for storytelling and a monospace font reserved for technical specs — rather than mixing fonts without reason.
This keeps every document, page, and product feeling like it belongs to the same brand.

## 05. Visual Language Help

Visual Language covers everything about your look beyond the logo and color palette — the imagery, graphic elements, and layout style that make your materials instantly recognizable.
Describe your preferred imagery style, supporting graphic elements, layout approach, and a short list of overall visual characteristics.
Be specific and consistent throughout, down to details like "no staged studio imagery" and "topographic contour patterns," reinforcing the brand rather than making generic design choices.
This is what keeps every photo, icon, and layout feeling like part of one coherent brand.

## 06. Brand Applications Help

Brand Applications translate your visual identity into practical guidance for each channel — website, social media, documents, products, and marketing materials.
For each one, describe what applying the brand well actually looks like in that specific context.
Don't just repeat the brand traits; adapt them per channel, like "minimal promotional messaging" for social media versus "lead with stories before products" for marketing materials.
This section is what makes your identity usable day-to-day by everyone producing content.

## 07. Brand Rules Help

Brand Rules are the non-negotiables that keep the brand consistent no matter who's creating content.
List what should always be done, what should never be done, and any approved exceptions.
Keep exceptions narrow and controlled, such as allowing seasonal colors for limited campaigns while keeping the core identity intact, rather than leaving the door open too wide.
Clear rules here prevent the slow drift that happens when everyone interprets the brand slightly differently.

## 08. Asset Inventory Help

The Asset Inventory is your central catalog of every official brand file — logos, icons, fonts, graphics, and related documents — so nothing gets lost or recreated from scratch.
List what exists in each category, and record exactly where everything is stored.
Be specific about both the files and their formats, like "Primary Logo (SVG, PNG, PDF)," and name the actual storage systems in use.
Keeping this current is what lets anyone on your team find the right asset without asking around.
