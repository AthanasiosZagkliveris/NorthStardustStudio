# 31. Positioning categories: collected data (owner, 2026-10-27)

Status: **collection only.** The owner's categories for the buyer, the user and the purpose of an offering, written down as given, before any question or layout is designed. **Nothing in the app follows from this document yet, and nothing will until the structure and the layout are agreed** (owner, 2026-10-27: "do not do any changes before complete the proper structure/layout; for now just collect some data / categories, and then we are going to create the proper questions and layout"). The wording, the numbers and the order are the owner's; the only edits are the layout of the lists and the spelling of "Seniors". Section 9 lists what the collection leaves open; it decides nothing.

Related: doc 28, 8.2 (the Positioning questions), doc 29 (the question guide), doc 30 (age limits by activity and country).

## 1. User age stages

- **Up to 18: Minors**
  - **Up to 3: Baby**
    - Up to 12 months: Infancy / Babyhood
    - 1 to 3 years: Toddlerhood
  - **3 to 11: Child**
    - 3 to 5 years: Early Childhood / Preschool
    - 5 to 11 years: Middle Childhood / Grade School
  - **11 to 18: Teenager**
    - 11 to 13 years: Early Adolescence
    - 13 to 16 years: Mid Adolescence
    - 16 to 18 years: Late Adolescence
- **18 to 65: Working Adult**
  - 18 to 25: Young
  - 25 to 40: Established
  - 40 to 55: Peak
  - 55 to 65: Mature
- **65 and over: Seniors**
  - 65 to 74: Active Retirees
  - 74 and over: Dependent seniors

## 2. Buyer's age stages

The owner's list stopped at the number "1." and was followed by this block, kept as pasted:

**Direct Buyers** (purchasing exclusively for their own personal use)

- **a. Fully Autonomous Buyers** (Working Adults, Active Retirees), 18 to 74 years
  - System traits: full legal capacity; holds credit, debit or bank accounts; enters into long-term contracts.
- **b. Semi-Autonomous Micro-Buyers** (Late Adolescents, 16 to 18)
  - System traits: age-restricted purchasing power; operates primarily via cash, digital wallets or gift cards; legally restricted from subscription contracts or credit lines.

(The other buyer stages, if any, are not written yet.)

## 3. Accessibility and user needs (dynamic cross-sections)

- **By duration**
  - Temporary needs (short-term, acute recovery, situational)
  - Permanent needs (long-term, chronic, stable, or progressive conditions)
- **By functional category**
  - Physical and mobility needs (wheelchair users, motor coordination, fine motor limitations)
  - Sensory needs (visual impairments, hearing loss, sensory processing sensitivities)
  - Cognitive and neurodivergent needs (ADHD, autism, learning differences, age-related cognitive slowing)

## 4. Caregiving and family life roles (universal cross-sections)

- **By dependent category** (who are they caring for?)
  - Childcare provider (parent, legal guardian, or foster parent of minors)
  - Eldercare provider (caring for aging parents, grandparents, or senior relatives)
  - Specialized care provider (caring for a spouse, sibling, or child with permanent or temporary medical or disability needs)
- **By role intensity and structure**
  - Sole / primary caregiver (the main person responsible for daily survival and decisions)
  - Co-caregiver / secondary support (shares the load with a partner, family network, or professional aides)
  - The "sandwich generation" caregiver (simultaneously managing multi-generational care, for example children and elders)
- **By household and legal dynamic**
  - Single-parent / solo-caregiver household (no co-parent or co-caregiver present in the home)
  - Multigenerational household (living with extended family where care is structurally distributed)
  - Kinship caregiver / youth caregiver (minors or young relatives acting as heads of household or primary supports)

## 5. Buyer categories

1. **Direct Buyers** (purchasing exclusively for their own personal use)
2. **Proxy Buyers** (purchasing exclusively on behalf of someone else)
3. **Dual-Intent Buyers** (purchasing a single offering to be used by themselves and their dependents)

## 6. Buyer and user relationships, with examples

Practical examples for each buyer and user relationship, using real-world organisational scenarios across B2C, B2B, B2G and G2B.

### 6.1 Personal relationships

| Relationship | Example |
|---|---|
| Parent or legal guardian | A parent buys a maths-learning app for their child. |
| Caregiver or person responsible for their care | A caregiver purchases adaptive equipment for an older adult they support. |
| Family member or relative | Someone buys a digital photo album subscription for a sibling. |
| Spouse or romantic partner | A person buys a watch or a couples' experience as a gift for their partner. |
| Friend or acquaintance | Someone buys an art workshop voucher for a friend. |
| Other personal relationship | A mentor purchases a career-planning course for a mentee. |

### 6.2 Business and professional relationships

| Relationship | Example |
|---|---|
| Employer providing for employees | A company purchases health and safety training for its staff. |
| Business partner or co-owner | One business partner purchases project-management software for another partner to use. |
| Business serving its clients or customers | An accountancy firm purchases financial-planning software to provide a service to its clients. |
| Professional intermediary acting for a client | An architect purchases a visualisation service on behalf of a property developer. |
| Sponsor or funder | A company funds access to a coding programme for selected participants. |
| Other business relationship | A franchise owner purchases training materials for franchise operators. |

### 6.3 Government and public-sector relationships

| Relationship | Example |
|---|---|
| Public authority providing for employees | A local council purchases case-management software for its social workers. |
| Public authority providing for citizens or residents | A municipality funds access to a public transport service for residents. |
| Public authority providing for service recipients or beneficiaries | A government agency commissions vocational training for unemployed people. |
| Public authority acting through another institution or service provider | A government department funds a charity to deliver support services to vulnerable households. |
| Other institutional relationship | A public library purchases accessible reading software for its registered users. |

### 6.4 Government-to-business relationships

These examples are slightly different because G2B often involves a government service or regulatory obligation rather than a conventional purchase.

| Relationship | Example |
|---|---|
| Government serving business owners or operators | A business owner uses a government portal to register a new company. |
| Government serving employees or authorised representatives | A company's authorised accountant submits employment records through a government portal. |
| Government providing regulatory or compliance services | A manufacturer uses a government system to submit mandatory safety documentation. |
| Government supporting or funding businesses | A small business applies for a government grant through an official funding portal. |
| Other institutional relationship | A business uses a government-provided export advisory service. |

## 7. Proposed offering purpose taxonomy

1. **Learning and Development.** Purpose: acquire knowledge, develop skills, or support personal, academic or professional growth. Examples: educational toys, courses, tutoring, employee training, professional development.
2. **Health, Care and Wellbeing.** Purpose: maintain or improve physical, mental or social wellbeing, or provide care and assistance. Examples: care services, accessibility equipment, fitness programmes, wellbeing support, assisted-living services.
3. **Gifting and Appreciation.** Purpose: express affection, gratitude, recognition, celebration or congratulations. Examples: personalised gifts, gift vouchers, employee rewards, corporate gifts, celebration packages.
4. **Entertainment and Enjoyment.** Purpose: provide pleasure, amusement, recreation or enjoyable experiences. Examples: games, films, toys, events, creative workshops, recreational subscriptions.
5. **Social Connection and Relationships.** Purpose: build, maintain or strengthen relationships and shared experiences. Examples: couples' activities, family experiences, community platforms, team-building events.
6. **Productivity and Efficiency.** Purpose: complete tasks more effectively, save time, or improve the use of resources. Examples: productivity software, workflow automation, business tools, administrative services.
7. **Business Growth and Performance.** Purpose: increase revenue, reach customers, improve competitiveness or strengthen organisational performance. Examples: marketing services, business consultancy, sales platforms, analytics, growth programmes.
8. **Creation and Expression.** Purpose: create, customise, communicate or express ideas, identity and creativity. Examples: design tools, art materials, custom products, creative software, branding services.
9. **Everyday Needs and Convenience.** Purpose: meet routine needs, reduce effort or make daily activities easier. Examples: household products, meal delivery, cleaning services, transport, everyday subscriptions.
10. **Protection and Risk Reduction.** Purpose: prevent harm, reduce uncertainty, protect assets or improve security. Examples: insurance, cybersecurity, safety equipment, backup services, childproofing products.
11. **Compliance and Obligations.** Purpose: meet legal, regulatory, contractual or institutional requirements. Examples: tax services, regulatory reporting, certification, mandatory training, licensing support.
12. **Access and Social Support.** Purpose: enable access to resources, opportunities or services that people or organisations might otherwise lack. Examples: scholarships, subsidised services, community support, accessibility solutions, employment assistance.

## 9. What the collection leaves open (for the design step; nothing is decided here)

1. **Age boundaries.** The stages share edges: Baby "up to 3" and Toddlerhood "1 to 3"; Child "3 to 11" with Middle Childhood "5 to 11"; Teenager from 11 while Early Adolescence is "11 to 13"; 18, 25, 40, 55, 65 and 74 each end one stage and start the next. A rule is needed for which stage an exact age belongs to (for example "from, up to but not including"). The doc 28 bands (0 to 2, 3 to 5, 6 to 9, 10 to 12, 13 to 15, 16 to 17, 18 to 24, 25 to 34 and so on) and these stages do not coincide.
2. **Minors, working adults and seniors overlap other lists.** "Late Adolescents 16 to 18" is both a user stage (1) and a buyer stage (2b); "Active Retirees 65 to 74" is part of Seniors (1) and of the Fully Autonomous Buyers (2a, 18 to 74). Whether a buyer stage and a user stage are one list or two lists that share names is not stated.
3. **The Buyer's age stages (2) are incomplete:** the numbered list is empty and only the Direct Buyers block (a, b) was given. The other buyer categories (5: Proxy Buyers, Dual-Intent Buyers) have no age-stage detail yet; whether a minor may buy on behalf of someone else varies by country (doc 30), which the design may need.
4. **The mapping table (8) has 11 cells for 12 purposes in every row.** Which purpose is the missing one is not stated: the cells may be shifted, or the last column (Access & support) may be blank in all rows. The table is kept as pasted and must be confirmed before anything reads it.
5. **Relationship lists (6) and the Positioning answers.** The 22 relationships of 6 (six personal, six business, five public sector, five government-to-business) sit under the four relationship models of Early Positioning (B2C, B2B, B2G, G2B Context), each with an "Other" row. The mapping table (8) lists 15 relationships, fewer than the 22 of 6. The buyer categories of 5 (Direct, Proxy, Dual-Intent) are a different cut from "Proxy Buyers / Gifters" in the life-stage question; how they relate is for the design.
6. **Accessibility (3) and caregiving (4) are "cross-sections"** of the user and the buyer, not stages: whether they are questions, tags on a Profile, or both is for the design.
7. **The earlier attempt.** The four End User questions built on 2026-10-26 (doc 28, 8.2, I7d to I7g) were a smaller, earlier structure; whether they stay, change or go once this structure is agreed is for the owner.
8. **Sources.** None of these lists has a source yet (rule 14: plain lists need a business-school or university document, classifications an official one). The age-stage names (Infancy, Toddlerhood, Early Childhood, Middle Childhood, Early, Mid and Late Adolescence) are developmental-psychology terms and can be sourced; the buyer and purpose taxonomies are the owner's.
